CHAPTER SIXTEEN

The Tools.

[PAUSE]

I have, until this chapter, deliberately kept the tools out of the foreground. Thirteen chapters about creative AI (Ay-Eye) without a chapter on the toolchain is, on the face of it, a strange editorial decision, and I want to begin by explaining it.

[PAUSE]

[calm] The reason is that I think the most common mistake people make about this period is to confuse the tools with the transition. Tools are the visible surface of the change. The thing the press cycle covers. The thing the platform companies want you to talk about. The thing that has a price and a logo and a launch date you can put on a slide.

[PAUSE]

The transition is everything underneath. The economics, the labour, the audience contract, the law, the institutions, the rails. The tools change weekly. The transition is slower, deeper, and is what will still be true in twenty thirty, when most of the tools in this chapter are obsolete.

[PAUSE]

The first sixteen chapters were about the transition. This chapter is about the tools.

[PAUSE]

I have written it last on purpose. Read in this order, the tools sit inside the architecture the book has been building. The Continuum, the Slop Ceiling, the four positions, the orchestrator role, the four principles. Read in any other order, they collapse back into the format the platform companies prefer. A tools-arms-race in which the only question is which model is best this week.

[PAUSE]

[deadpan] That format is, in twenty twenty-six, the most reliable way to misunderstand what is happening.

[PAUSE]

A note on the date stamp. Everything in this chapter is current to June twenty twenty-six. The May twenty twenty-six cut of the catalogue, with the Issue Thirty-One additions to the video, music, world-model, agent and games-engine layers folded in where they fit. By the time you read it, some of these tools will have been bought, renamed, killed, surpassed or repositioned.

[PAUSE]

The point is not that the specific tools matter. The point is the shape of the toolchain. What categories exist, what they do, who builds them, and how a working creative builds a coherent stack on top of them. The shape, in my experience, holds.

[LONG PAUSE]

How to think about the toolchain.

[PAUSE]

Before the inventory, the frame.

[PAUSE]

I think the creative-AI toolchain in twenty twenty-six is best understood as a stack of seven layers, each with its own dominant players, its own pace of change, its own integration model. The layers, from foundation to consumer, are these.

[PAUSE]

First. Foundation models. The large multimodal systems underneath everything else. Open A.I. (Open-Ay-Eye)'s G.P.T.-class models, Anthropic (An-throp-ic)'s Claude (rhymes with load), Google's Gemini (Jem-in-ee), Meta's Llama, the major Chinese open-source models. These are the raw capability layer. Almost no working creative uses them directly except via wrappers.

[PAUSE]

Second. Modality models. Specialist models for video, image, audio, three-D and world. For video — Sora (Sore-ah), Veo (Vay-oh), Runway Gen Four Point Five, Kling, Hunyuan, Wan. For image — Firefly, Midjourney (Mid-journey), Flux, Imagen (Imagine), and the Stable Diffusion variants including S-D-X-L. For audio — Suno (Soo-no), Udio (Oo-dee-oh), Eleven Labs, Mureka. For three-D and world — Marble, Genie Three, WorldGen, UNI-One, Hunyuan Three-D PolyGen, ECHO. These are what most working creatives think of when they say "AI tools."

[PAUSE]

Third. Agent platforms. Systems that compose modality models and external tools into multi-step workflows. Open A.I.'s AgentKit, Anthropic's Claude apps and skills, Google's Project Genie, HeyGen (Hey-gen)'s Video Agent, Sony's forty-nine-agent, seventy-two-skill stack. The agent layer is where the orchestrator economy of Chapter Eleven actually runs.

[PAUSE]

Fourth. Creative software with AI baked in. The legacy creative suites that have been rebuilt as AI-first platforms. Adobe Creative Cloud, Autodesk, Foundry, Unreal Engine, Unity, Davinci Resolve, Pro Tools, Logic Pro, Ableton. This is where most paid professional work still happens.

[PAUSE]

Fifth. AI-native creative apps. New entrants whose product is a single-purpose AI workflow. Runway, Higgsfield (Higgs-field), Krea (Cray-ah), Freepik, Magnific, Synthesia (Sin-thee-zee-ah), HeyGen, Hedra, Cascadeur, Pika (Pee-kah), Luma (Loom-ah). Most working creatives use four to ten of these in rotation.

[PAUSE]

Sixth. Open-source and workflow infrastructure. The technical-creator layer that wires everything together. ComfyUI (Comfy-You-Eye), Hugging Face, SuperSplat, OpenEnv, the open-source model ecosystem. This is where the most interesting innovation often happens first.

[PAUSE]

Seventh. Consumer surfaces. The apps that put generative capability on every phone. The Sora app, CapCut and Dreamina with Seedance, the Gemini app, the various TikTok-style remix platforms. This is the layer the audience touches.

[PAUSE]

The mistake I see most often, both in the press cycle and in studios planning their internal AI roadmaps, is to optimise for layer two — modality models — without understanding that the actual leverage is in how you compose layers two, three, four and six into a coherent workflow. [deadpan] The tool that wins is rarely the tool with the best benchmark. It is the tool that integrates cleanly into the rest of your stack.

[PAUSE]

With that frame, the inventory.

[LONG PAUSE]

Video.

[PAUSE]

The video layer changed faster than any other modality between October twenty twenty-five and June twenty twenty-six, and is the one most likely to look different again by the time you read this. Treat the names as snapshots, not as a stable league table.

[PAUSE]

Sora Two, from Open A.I., is the model that opened the period this book covers. Its September twenty twenty-five launch — physical realism, audio integration, multi-shot world-state persistence — is the moment Chapter One is about. The iOS app launched alongside it hit a million downloads in five days. By March twenty twenty-six, however, the platform was shutting down. Open A.I. announced a staged wind-down on the twenty-fourth of March twenty twenty-six. Consumer app and website ending late April. Developer A.P.I. closing late September. Peak revenue reportedly at around five hundred and forty thousand dollars per month against operating costs in the billions, and compute reallocated toward higher-margin coding and enterprise products.

[PAUSE]

The Disney and Open A.I. one billion dollar licensing announcement of the eleventh of December twenty twenty-five was never executed. No formal agreement was signed and no money changed hands. The contractual framework underneath that deal — I.P.-not-training, talent-likeness excluded, jointly-governed steering committee, equity-and-warrants structure — survived the platform's collapse and is now the reference architecture other studios negotiate against. The deep treatment of that architecture, and the broader I.P. (Eye-Pee)-economics shift it sits inside, is in Appendix L, The Programmable Brand.

[PAUSE]

For working filmmakers in mid twenty twenty-six, the practical implication of the Sora collapse is that the consumer-facing edge of the AI-video market has rotated to ByteDance's CapCut, Dreamina and Seedance stack, to the YouTube Shorts integrations of Veo Three Point One, and to the Sora app's various TikTok-and-Reels-shaped successors. The professional pipeline never depended on Sora Two in the first place. Most working filmmakers I know used it less than its cultural prominence suggested. And so on the production side the collapse changes less than the headlines implied.

[PAUSE]

Veo Three Point One, from Google DeepMind, released in mid-October twenty twenty-five, is the model the professional filmmaking community has, on average, gravitated toward. For narrative coherence, controllable camera composition, cinematic lighting vocabulary and sound integration. Sora Two wins on raw physics in single clips. Veo Three Point One wins on the kind of sustained directorial control most actual production pipelines need.

[PAUSE]

Runway Gen Four Point Five — and Gen Four Point Five Image-to-Video, the Workflows product, Story Panels, Characters A.P.I. (Ay-Pee-Eye), Apps for Advertising — is the most-integrated commercial AI-video stack of the period. Runway has shipped product faster than any other AI video company in this market, and CEO Cristobal Valenzuela's "fifty indie films instead of one hundred million dollar blockbuster" framing is the cleanest articulation I have seen of the case for AI as creative leverage rather than cost-cut.

[PAUSE]

Then there is the Chinese-built tier. Kling, from Kuaishou. Hunyuan Video, from Tencent. Wan Two Point Five, from Alibaba. Seedance Two Point Zero, from ByteDance. These models, in aggregate, have rivalled or surpassed the U.S. labs on specific capabilities — motion physics, character consistency, render speed — at significantly lower cost. Hunyuan's open-source releases have been the single most important contribution to the wider open-source AI video ecosystem in this period.

[PAUSE]

Pika Two Point Zero, Higgsfield and Luma — with Dream Machine and UNI-One — round out the commercial layer. Each has carved a niche. Pika on iteration speed and creator workflow. Higgsfield on social-media marketing video at scale — eighty million dollars raised, one point three billion dollar valuation, two hundred million dollars revenue in nine months. And Luma on the world-model bridge to spatial content.

[PAUSE]

HeyGen ships Video Agent, a full scripting-to-assembly agent built around reference images. Synthesia holds the corporate AI-avatar market, with a four billion dollar valuation, having reportedly rejected a three billion dollar Adobe acquisition offer. Eleven Labs runs the dominant audio-AI layer underneath much of the new video work, at five hundred million dollar annual recurring revenue by April twenty twenty-six.

[PAUSE]

Gemini Omni, from Google DeepMind, announced at Google I/O twenty twenty-six, brings text, image, audio, video and live interaction into a single multimodal model. The first foundation-model release in this category that meaningfully unifies the modalities working creatives currently have to bridge across five different tools. Beeple Canvas, Mike Winkelmann's gen-AI compositor, launched May twenty twenty-six, is the first AI-native compositing application to ship from a working visual-effects artist's own studio. And is structurally distinct from the AI-features-bolted-onto-existing-compositors pattern in the legacy-software section below.

[PAUSE]

If I had to name a single video product that, in my experience, working creatives have settled on as a default in twenty twenty-six, it would be Veo Three Point One for finished work and Runway for iteration and integration. Sora is the brand name the audience knows. The actual production pipelines run on the other two.

[LONG PAUSE]

Image.

[PAUSE]

The image layer is more stable than video. The technology has matured, the differences between top models are narrower, and the dominant question has moved from "which model" to "which workflow."

[PAUSE]

Adobe Firefly — Image Model Five, plus Foundry for custom-trained corporate models, plus integration across Photoshop, Illustrator, Express and InDesign — is the default for any working creative who is also a Creative Cloud subscriber. Which is, by Adobe's own numbers, forty-five percent of Creative Cloud users actively using Firefly, seventy percent of those weekly, more than twenty-two billion assets generated by April twenty twenty-five. The Firefly adoption curve is the single best evidence I have for the consumption-gap argument in Chapter Thirteen.

[PAUSE]

Midjourney remains the aesthetic-leadership product in the category. Slower to ship, more opinionated about output style, dominant on Discord and X among the working AI-art community.

[PAUSE]

Flux, from Black Forest Labs, is the open-source and pro-creator favourite for fine control, having largely replaced Stable Diffusion X-L as the open-weight default through twenty twenty-five.

[PAUSE]

Google Imagen — and the Nano Banana fast-image variant integrated into Gemini, Photoshop and Unreal Engine via the various plugins — has become the most-integrated image model in the consumer toolchain, by virtue of Google's distribution. Nano Banana inside Photoshop and Nano Banana inside Unreal Engine were two of the more consequential cross-platform integrations of the period.

[PAUSE]

Krea, Freepik, Magnific, Recraft — the higher-control consumer and pro-creator products built on top of foundation image models. Each is competing on specific workflow advantages. Real-time generation, upscaling, vector output, brand-consistency control.

[PAUSE]

The image workflow most commonly cited in my circle in mid twenty twenty-six is this. A base generation in Firefly, Midjourney or Flux. Character-consistent variation in a controllable wrapper like Krea or Magnific. Finishing inside Photoshop, with the AI-assisted masking, generative-fill and object-removal tools that Adobe shipped through the autumn twenty twenty-five and spring twenty twenty-six update cycle.

[LONG PAUSE]

Music and audio.

[PAUSE]

The music layer split into three categories during this period, and the split is, in my experience, more important than the specific products in each category.

[PAUSE]

First, generative music tools that produce finished tracks from prompts. Suno, with Studio launched late twenty twenty-five. Udio. And Mureka, with its Music Agent Studio — six specialised AI agents for songwriting, arrangement and production. These are the tools that produce most of the AI-music flood Chapter Five describes. They are also, paradoxically, the tools most working musicians use the least directly. The consumer market for AI-generated finished tracks is large and growing, but professional musicians overwhelmingly use AI tools at a different layer.

[PAUSE]

Second, production and post-production AI. The tools that handle audio restoration, mixing, mastering and isolation. The eleven hundred-creator music survey discussed in Appendix D found that fifty-eight percent of professional producers used AI for audio restoration, thirty-eight percent for mixing assistance, thirty-three point nine percent for automated mastering. iZotope Ozone Twelve, LANDR, the Pro Tools and Logic Pro AI suites, Cleanvoice A.I. for podcast post-production — this is where the silent-adoption majority of the music industry lives.

[PAUSE]

Third, voice and audio synthesis. Eleven Labs is the dominant player, with five hundred million dollar A.R.R., BlackRock and NVIDIA backing, and meaningful share across audiobook narration, dubbing, podcast synthesis and AI character voice work. The Cardiff band that found their music had been used to train an "AI artist" outperforming them on Spotify is one of the cautionary tales of this layer. The Andrii Daniels bomb-shelter clip is one of the success cases.

[PAUSE]

Sound-effect foundation models emerged as a new sub-category in May twenty twenty-six. Sony A.I.'s Woosh is the first foundation model explicitly trained for the professional sound-effects discipline. Built for the people who design the sonic worlds behind games, film and interactive media, not for the consumer market. Mirelo S-F-X One Point Six shipped the first sound-effects model that lets you edit a generated sound rather than only regenerate it. A structural shift in the discipline equivalent to the move from rendered images to layered Photoshop files. Stable Audio Three Point Zero, from Stability AI, shipped as an open-weight audio model family explicitly aimed at artistic experimentation. Tamber, the ethically-trained AI music suite I describe in Chapter Six, shipped alongside a gestural-control interface that lets the musician steer the generation with arm movements. And Beatport's Track ID rolled out as the real-time identification standard for the D.J. market.

[PAUSE]

The deal flow underneath this layer is the second-fastest-changing in the toolchain. The Stability AI and Universal Music alliance. The Stability AI and Warner Music deal. The Splice and Universal partnership. The GEMA and Open A.I. lawsuit. The Wixen and Meta lawsuit. The U-M-G and Anthropic three billion dollar suit. These are the structural moves I would track if I were a working musician trying to plan a five-year toolchain.

[LONG PAUSE]

Three-D, world models, spatial.

[PAUSE]

The category that, more than any other, I think defines the next decade of creative work. Chapter Eight is the long-form argument. This section is the inventory.

[PAUSE]

Marble, from World Labs — Fei-Fei Li (Fay-Fay Lee)'s company — is the first commercial product I would put on a professional toolchain. Public release November twenty twenty-five. Sony Pictures' use of it in virtual production reportedly running forty times faster than the legacy workflow. DreamLab has been in the beta since October twenty twenty-five, and Marble is, today, the world-model product most integrated into a working pipeline I have used.

[PAUSE]

Google DeepMind Genie Three is the most ambitious research-grade world model, named by Time as one of the best inventions of twenty twenty-five. Made publicly available to Google AI Ultra subscribers via Project Genie in January twenty twenty-six.

[PAUSE]

Meta WorldGen. Tencent H-Y World One Point Five, open-sourced December twenty twenty-five alongside the Hunyuan Three-D Studio integration. SpAItial ECHO. Stanford Wonderzoom. OpenArt Worlds. Luma UNI-One, the most important category announcement of spring twenty twenty-six, combining world generation with reasoning. These are the rest of the world-model commercial layer.

[PAUSE]

The May twenty twenty-six world-model wave extended this layer further. NVIDIA SANA-W-M is the first open-weight world model at meaningful scale — two point six billion parameters — with sixty-second video generation and explicit camera control. Odyssey Starchild-One is, by Odyssey's own framing, the first ever real-time multimodal world model. A system that doesn't just generate a world but simulates and reasons about it. Odyssey Agora-One is the multiplayer companion to Starchild, putting four players inside the same AI-generated world, built — in a small piece of provenance theatre — on the bones of a nineteen ninety-seven shooter. And Apple Headsup is a research-grade three-D Gaussian head-reconstruction pipeline built for multi-view captures from consumer iPhones. Extending the Vision-Pro-Personas Gaussian-splat thread into the open research layer.

[PAUSE]

Underneath this layer, the Gaussian-splatting infrastructure has matured into a stable workflow. SuperSplat, from PlayCanvas, for editing. Spark Two Point Zero for open-source streaming of one-hundred-million-splat scenes to browsers. And the S-O-G slash WebP equivalent compression standard. Apple's confirmation that its Vision Pro Personas feature is powered by Gaussian splatting under the hood made it, by some margin, the most-deployed Gaussian-splat technology in consumer hardware as of late twenty twenty-five.

[PAUSE]

For the three-D-asset and material side, the rapidly-maturing three-D-asset generation layer is being integrated, model-by-model, into Unreal Engine, Unity and Blender pipelines. Hunyuan Three-D PolyGen One Point Five — Tencent's "art-grade" three-D generative model. Hitem3D. Meshy. Rodin.

[PAUSE]

Ubisoft's open-sourcing of its CHORD P-B-R-material model in December twenty twenty-five, and the Blender Foundation's patronage deal with Anthropic announced in May twenty twenty-six, are two of the more strategically significant moves in this layer. Both pushing the production-grade open-source tooling forward at a pace the commercial alternatives have struggled to match.

[LONG PAUSE]

Agent platforms and orchestration.

[PAUSE]

The category I think most working creatives are still underestimating, six months after Chapter Three argued it was the inflection point of the era.

[PAUSE]

Open A.I. AgentKit — Agent Builder, ChatKit, connector registry, eval framework — launched October twenty twenty-five and is the developer-facing platform underneath most third-party agentic creative tools.

[PAUSE]

Anthropic Claude apps, and the Skills framework — the system of named, reusable capabilities that Claude Code uses to coordinate multi-agent workflows. The Sony forty-nine-agent, seventy-two-skill game-development stack is built on this. In May twenty twenty-six, Google released its own official skills for AI agents. A parallel, cross-vendor skills layer that lets Google-side agents do what Anthropic's Skills framework has been doing for Claude-side ones. The convergence of two named skills frameworks across the foundation-model vendors is, in my read, the first sign that the orchestration layer is settling on a shared vocabulary rather than continuing to fragment.

[PAUSE]

Tencent Ardot, the company's AI-native design-agent platform launched May twenty twenty-six, is the most ambitious non-Western agent-platform launch of the period. An integrated environment in which generative design agents handle layout, asset generation, brand application and iteration as a single coordinated pipeline. In the same week, Viktor raised seventy-five million dollars to embed an agentic coworker directly into Slack and Microsoft Teams. The agentic layer landing not as a standalone product but as a colleague-shaped presence in the chat surface the working creative is already in all day, as discussed in Chapter Nine.

[PAUSE]

HeyGen Video Agent, for end-to-end video assembly. Adobe C-X Enterprise, announced at Adobe Summit twenty twenty-six with NVIDIA, for agentic creative intelligence across the full content lifecycle. NVIDIA plus Google Cloud, for the underlying creative-AI infrastructure most enterprise pipelines run on.

[PAUSE]

ComfyUI — the open-source node-based workflow editor — sits underneath much of the technical-creator community's agentic work. ComfyUI raised seventeen million dollars in October twenty twenty-five and hit a five hundred million dollar valuation by May twenty twenty-six. The platform has become the de facto operating system for the open-source side of the creative-AI ecosystem. In May twenty twenty-six, Anthropic's Claude was added as an official partner node inside ComfyUI, joining the existing Google, Open A.I. and open-weight nodes. Meaning the three frontier foundation models can now be orchestrated side-by-side inside the same open-source pipeline.

[PAUSE]

Hugging Face. OpenEnv, from Meta and Hugging Face. The Hugging Face and Google Cloud partnership. The open-source agentic-development infrastructure.

[PAUSE]

For working creatives, the practical agent stack in twenty twenty-six is some combination of these four. First, a foundation model — Claude, G.P.T. or Gemini — for the orchestration brain. Second, a modality model layer — video, image, audio, three-D — doing the actual generation. Third, a workflow integration layer — ComfyUI for technical work, the in-app agent surfaces for less technical work. And fourth, a judgement layer. The human at the desk, doing what Chapter Eleven calls briefing, allocating, judging and integrating.

[PAUSE]

The team I work with at DreamLab runs this stack in production every week. The agents that handle our daily work in May twenty twenty-six are, in aggregate, doing the labour of what would, two years ago, have been a team three to four times our size. [deadpan] The human team has not shrunk. We have just become substantially more leveraged.

[LONG PAUSE]

Legacy creative software, repositioned.

[PAUSE]

The most under-reported strategic story of this period, in my view, has been the speed at which the legacy creative-software vendors have rebuilt their products as AI-agent platforms.

[PAUSE]

Adobe. I have written enough about Adobe in Chapter Nine that I will not repeat it here. The short version. Creative Cloud is, today, a stack of AI agents wearing a Photoshop, Premiere, After Effects, Illustrator, InDesign and Acrobat skin. The agents are inside the apps. The apps are inside ChatGPT. The apps are inside Adobe Express. The apps are inside the new C-X Enterprise platform. The repositioning is complete.

[PAUSE]

Unreal Engine, from Epic. The games engine that has, through plugins, integrations and the Nano Banana and Gemini partnership, become a hybrid game-engine, virtual-production and AI-generation hub. The Unreal Engine Five AI Assistant, announced at the end of twenty twenty-five, is one of the more consequential single-product launches of the period. The ECABridge connector, launched May twenty twenty-six, is the most-cited Unreal-Engine M.C.P. (Em-See-Pee) integration of the spring. Providing the Model Context Protocol surface and a set of agentic capabilities Epic itself has not yet shipped to the launcher. In a separate but related move, an Epic Games veteran announced an AI-heavy, quote, "Fully European" game-engine project in the same week. The first plausibly-credible new entrant in the triple-A game-engine market since the early twenty-tens, framed explicitly around AI as the core operating layer.

[PAUSE]

Unity. Unity's AI Open Beta, in May twenty twenty-six. An in-editor AI suite for the full games-development pipeline, alongside the company's AI Council formation in October twenty twenty-five.

[PAUSE]

Autodesk, Foundry, SideFX. The V-F-X (Vee-Eff-Ex)-pipeline vendors integrating generative AI into Maya, Nuke and Houdini (Hoo-dee-nee) at the speed the VFX industry's adoption curve — sixty-two percent of Hollywood studios on automated compositing, thirty-five percent reduction in post-production timelines — demanded.

[PAUSE]

Blender. Open-source three-D, now a recognised industry-grade tool, beneficiary of the Anthropic Foundation patronage deal.

[PAUSE]

Davinci Resolve, from Blackmagic. Avid Media Composer. Pro Tools. The editorial and audio post environments, all now shipping AI-augmented features that have become baseline expectations.

[PAUSE]

The thing to note is that the legacy software did not get displaced by the AI-native products. The legacy software absorbed the AI-native capability and kept the underlying user community. Adobe was supposed to lose to Midjourney in twenty twenty-four. [deadpan] Adobe is, instead, the dominant generative-AI player by aggregate creator engagement in twenty twenty-six. The platform companies bet on this absorption pattern, and that bet has, so far, paid off.

[LONG PAUSE]

Open source.

[PAUSE]

The open-source ecosystem has, against the odds and against most VC predictions in twenty twenty-four, held its ground through this period. And is, in several categories, the leader rather than the follower.

[PAUSE]

Hugging Face. The operating system of open-source AI, expanding aggressively through twenty twenty-five and twenty twenty-six.

[PAUSE]

ComfyUI. Already discussed.

[PAUSE]

Open-source models from Tencent's Hunyuan, Alibaba's Qwen and Wan, DeepSeek, Meta's Llama, Mistral, Stability AI. Collectively, the open-weight ecosystem that, by the spring of twenty twenty-six, was being used by approximately eighty percent of startups pitching the Andreessen Horowitz fund. NVIDIA's SANA-W-M, in May twenty twenty-six, extended this list to world-models for the first time at meaningful parameter scale.

[PAUSE]

PhotoGIMP, the open-source skin that takes GIMP and makes it look and feel exactly like Photoshop, became, in this period, a credible Photoshop alternative for working creatives who wanted to opt out of the Adobe subscription stack. The open-source equivalent of the Tools I do not use discipline in the section above.

[PAUSE]

OpenEnv, from Meta and Hugging Face, for open-source agentic development. Korin AI — the Africa-trained, Africa-built model launched May twenty twenty-six. SuperSplat. Spark Two Point Zero. PlayCanvas S-O-G. Blender. The open-source spatial and three-D infrastructure layer.

[PAUSE]

If you are a working creative trying to build a long-term, defensible toolchain that does not depend on the unilateral pricing or policy decisions of a single platform company, the open-source ecosystem in twenty twenty-six is materially viable in a way it was not eighteen months ago. We have built significant parts of the DreamLab pipeline on top of it precisely for that reason.

[LONG PAUSE]

Tools I do not use, and why.

[PAUSE]

I want to be specific, because lists of "best tools" without exclusions are not useful.

[PAUSE]

I do not use AI tools whose terms of service claim ownership over my output, or that train on user inputs without an opt-out. Multiple consumer-facing AI products in this period have shipped with terms that working creatives should read carefully before adopting.

[PAUSE]

I do not use AI tools whose training data provenance I cannot, in some material way, verify or trust. The growing infrastructure for creative weight attribution, watermarking and C-Two-P-A compliance is, in my view, the right side of the market to be on. Tools that explicitly reject that infrastructure are tools that I have, increasingly, kept out of our production pipeline.

[PAUSE]

I do not use the AI products that have made the most noise in the consumer press cycle. The marketing-driven launches — the products whose first appearance is a viral demo and whose second appearance is a Series-A round — are, in my experience, the products most likely to have collapsed or pivoted by the time you need them in production six months later.

[PAUSE]

I do not, finally, use AI tools to produce work in the disciplines where my own craft is the value I am bringing to the client. The Continuum frame from Chapter Three is, for me, a daily operational practice, not a theoretical model. The places I sit on the right edge of the line are deliberately chosen. The places I sit on the left are deliberately defended.

[LONG PAUSE]

The complete toolchain. A categorised reference.

[PAUSE]

This section is a reference inventory, not a recommendation list. It catalogues every tool, model, platform, app, plugin, LoRA, workflow and service that Dream Machine tracked across its thirty-two issues, from October twenty twenty-five to June twenty twenty-six. Some are dominant. Some are niche. Some have already been bought, renamed or discontinued by the time you read this. The point of the list is not "what to use." The point is what existed, in this period, in the creative-AI toolchain — so that the shape of the field is on the record.

[PAUSE]

A word on the list's grain. I have tried to err on the side of inclusion. Where a single company ships multiple closely-related products — Adobe's Sneaks portfolio, the Runway Gen Four Point Five family, the Qwen-Edit LoRA series — I have grouped them under the parent entry but called out the constituent tools. Because in this period each constituent shipped to working creatives separately and changed at its own cadence. Where a tool was a one-issue demo I could not later verify, I have still listed it. That the demo existed at all is part of the field's history. Where a tool's name conflicts with another — there are at least three things called "Wonder" in the period the book covers — I have annotated.

[PAUSE]

The list runs to roughly six hundred entries. Skim it. Use the categories. Come back to specific sections when you need them.

[LONG PAUSE]

Foundation models and Large Language Models.

[PAUSE]

The foundation-model layer begins with ChatGPT, G.P.T. Five and G.P.T. Five Pro from Open A.I. — the dominant consumer L.L.M. (Large Language Model) and reference foundation model, eight hundred to nine hundred million weekly active users, G.P.T. Five and G.P.T. Five Pro announced at DevDay twenty twenty-five.

[PAUSE]

Claude, Claude Code, Claude Apps and Claude Skills, from Anthropic. The writers' and developers' favoured second. Strong long-context performance. The agentic coding environment that underlies Sony's forty-nine-agent, seventy-two-skill stack. Claude for Legal launched May twenty twenty-six.

[PAUSE]

Gemini, Gemini Two Point Five Flash, Gemini Three and Gemini Three Point One Flash, from Google. Google's multimodal L.L.M. family. Desktop users growing one hundred and fifty-five percent year-on-year in twenty twenty-five to twenty twenty-six. Gemini Three Point One Flash T-T-S is the most controllable Google voice model as of spring twenty twenty-six.

[PAUSE]

Llama, from Meta — the dominant open-weight foundation model.

[PAUSE]

How to build a toolchain you can defend.

[PAUSE]

The last thing I want to say in this chapter is something I have said in talks more often than anything else, because working creatives ask me this question, in some variant, every week.

[PAUSE]

How do I decide what tools to use, in a market that is changing this fast, without burning my whole month re-learning interfaces?

[PAUSE]

My short answer is — build the toolchain in layers, and accept that the layers move at different speeds.

[PAUSE]

The bottom layer — your foundation model, your modality stack, your agent platform — will change frequently. Treat it as ephemeral. Pick the best tools available this quarter and be ready to swap them next quarter.

[PAUSE]

The middle layer — your creative software. Adobe, Unreal, Davinci, Pro Tools, Blender, Logic — will change more slowly, and is the layer in which the AI capability will be progressively absorbed. Treat it as the long-term home of your craft. Learn it deeply.

[PAUSE]

The top layer — your judgement, your taste, your briefing skill, your integration sense — does not change. It is the layer the agents cannot copy, the layer the platforms cannot ship, and the layer the next model release does not depreciate. Spend more time here than the toolchain wants you to.

[PAUSE]

The mistake I see working creatives make most often is to over-optimise the bottom layer and under-invest the top. The platform companies want you to spend your working hours chasing the new model release. The work that pays the bills, the work that finds an audience, and the work that survives a transition is built on the layer the platform companies cannot reach.

[PAUSE]

[calm] [EMPHASIS] The toolchain, in the end, is the means. The work is the end. The tools change. The work, if it is any good, lasts.

[PAUSE]

That is the working operating model my studio has run on for the period this book covers. It is the model I would commend to anyone building, this year or next, a creative practice that survives the rest of the decade.

[PAUSE]

[calm] The transition is going to keep going. The tools will keep changing. The work that matters, on the other side, will be made by the people who kept their attention on the right layer.
